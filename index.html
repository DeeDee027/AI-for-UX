01

Executive Summary

UX design looks different than it did three or four years ago. Back then, a UX designer's or product manager's primary focus was manual execution: wireframing, prototyping, and producing deliverables by hand. Today, AI can take on much of that execution, and the required skill set is shifting toward high-level design evaluation and functional prototyping.

That shift doesn't remove the designer from the process. AI can speed up nearly every stage of UX work, but it still needs human oversight to catch errors, question its output, and keep the user at the center of every decision.

This guide shows where that balance lies. Organized around the , it walks through each stage of the UX process, from discovery and research to prototyping and handoff. At each stage, it identifies which tasks AI can handle, which ones call for human judgment, and how to work with AI as a junior partner rather than a replacement.

02

AI as the Junior Partner

Ever since AI took off, there's one question I've heard more times than I can count. It comes from fellow UX designers, from web developers, and, honestly, from anyone whose job involves a computer: "Where do I fit into all this?" Or, more bluntly: "Is AI going to replace me?"

It's a question I ask myself too, from time to time.

Reasonable as the question is, I think the answer is quite simple. The fact that AI is quick on its feet, more than us humans are or will ever be, is not up for debate. However, without training, AI has no context about your users, and even with that training, it still can't read a room, and is sometimes confidently wrong.

Accordingly, the real question isn't whether AI will replace designers. It's how designers should work with it. The model I keep coming back to is the junior designer: someone who is fast, eager, tireless, and capable of producing a surprising amount of work, but who doesn't yet know your users, your business, or when something "technically okay" is actually wrong.

You wouldn't hand a junior designer a project and ship whatever comes back. You'd brief them clearly, give them context, review their work, and push back when something misses the mark. Working with AI is no different. This approach has a name: , meaning a person stays involved at the decision points rather than letting AI run unchecked from start to finish.

Three ground rules for working with your junior partner

  1. Brief it like it's a colleague. AI output is only as good as the AI's training and the context it gets. Vague prompts produce vague results.
  2. Verify everything. AI can and present made-up facts with absolute confidence. Treat its output as a first draft that needs tons of revisions and polishing, instead of a finished product.
  3. You own the outcome. If an AI-generated design excludes users or is biased against certain demographics, that is on you as a designer, not on the tool. You have to always ensure that the information the tool is given is accurate and representative of your users, while also ensuring that you don't paste into an AI tool your organization hasn't approved.

How to use this guide

The remaining chapters follow the four phases of the Double Diamond framework: Discover, Define, Develop, and Deliver. Each chapter shows which tasks to delegate, which to keep, a sample prompt you can adapt, and the risks to watch for.

03 Discover

Discovery & Research

Discovery is the process of going wide to understand users, their needs, pain points, the context in which they live and how all that shapes their experience with a system or product. During this phase, it can be very tempting to use AI because of how speedy its turnaround can be, but it's also arguably the most dangerous phase to use AI in, because everything that follows during the design process hinges on getting this stage right.

It's not entirely "wrong" to use AI during this stage, but it's recommended that the designer draws a clear picture of what parts of this process they should own and what parts AI can handle.

Who handles what during discovery
AI can handleThe designer should own
Drafting interview guides and survey questionsConducting interviews and reading body language, hesitation, and emotion
Summarizing interview transcriptsDeciding which insights actually matter
Converting notes from user interviews to themesSorting notes by the underlying meaning the users had
Conducting competitive scans and desk researchRecruiting interview participants

Sample prompt

You are a UX researcher helping me synthesize interview notes. Below you'll see anonymized notes from 6 interviews with frequent concert-goers who are top listeners of a certain artist on Spotify about how they decide whether to attend a concert with people they've met through an app. Group observations into 4-6 themes. For each theme, list the supporting observations and which participant they came from (P1-P6). Refrain from adding any observations that are not in the notes. If something is ambiguous, please flag it instead of attempting to interpret it.

The last two sentences are doing all the heavy lifting because contrary to the rest of the prompt which tells AI what tasks to do, the two sentences tell it what not to do, which narrows the space for AI hallucination thus making the output checkable.

04 Define

Problem Definition & Information Architecture

The Define phase compiles all your learnings from the user research and interviews into a clear problem statement, giving you a better idea of what it is that you need to work on solving. is useful as it gives your proposed solution a structure that users can easily navigate. While AI is helpful during certain parts of both processes like drafting problem statements from your user research, proposing personas, etc, it can't ultimately decide which option makes the most sense or solves the users' pain points in the most effective manner, which is why the designer should own certain parts of the process.

Who handles what during problem definition and IA
AI can handleThe designer should own
Drafting "How can we address…" statements from the user research themesChoosing what problem to prioritize solving
Drafting personasCross-checking personas against existing data and eliminating stereotypes
Drafting sitemaps and navigation optionsMaking IA decisions based on user mental models

Sample prompt

Based on the research themes below, propose two alternative information architectures for Concert Connect, an opt-in feature within the Spotify app that lets top listeners of an artist find others attending the same local concert. Themes: (1) safety comes first, (2) shared taste builds trust, and (3) users value privacy around listening data. For each option, explain the organizing logic (e.g., by concert, by match, by privacy setting), where the feature lives in the existing app, and one risk it might create for users. Use the same labels participants used in the research where applicable rather than internal jargon.

Watch out for

  • Generic personas: Without your research data, AI defaults to bland, stereotyped personas, such as "Khaya, 26, music lover who enjoys attending concerts and live shows." A persona like this could describe almost anyone on Spotify and is a common form of . A useful Concert Connect persona reflects what participants actually said: for example, a seasoned solo concert goer might be open to having company every now and then, but also have concerns around meeting strangers without knowing where and when first.
  • limits: If you paste in all six interview transcripts plus your themes and existing app structure, the AI may lose track of earlier material and recommend a structure that ignores the safety concerns previously voiced by users early on. A recommended best practice is to break large datasets into easy-to-process chunks and summarize relevant data as you go.
  • Use of internal jargon in labels: AI often reuses whatever terms you gave it. For instance, if you initially briefed it using a working name like "Listener Matching," your navigation may end up adopting that same label, even if participants may have described the idea as "finding people with a similar music taste to attend concerts with." Accordingly, labels should reflect how users talk, not the project team's internal jargon and references.
  • Privacy hidden in settings: AI often follows common app patterns, which usually means placing privacy controls under a general Settings menu. This might be a huge risk especially for a feature that shares listening data and concert plans with strangers. Participants in Theme 3 expressed a preference for privacy around what potential concert matches can see, an option which might necessitate an opt-in flow, which can give users the autonomy to control which data their potential matches can see.

05 Develop

Ideation, Wireframing & Copywriting

AI's ability to produce lots of information quickly makes it a strong brainstorming partner, but volume isn't necessarily synonymous with quality. This phase, like all its predecessors, can be streamlined with the help of AI, but human oversight is crucial at this stage as well.

Who handles what during ideation, wireframing, and copywriting
AI can handleThe designer should own
Generating concept directionsDeciding on what direction best aligns with users and business goals
Producing wireframe layoutsCrosschecking layouts against accessibility standards (WCAG)
Creating buttons, drafting error messages, empty statesEnsuring alignment with brand voice and guidelines
Drafting alt textChecking for errors, dark patterns, and misalignments

Sample prompt

Write 5 versions of the short explanation shown on the Concert Connect opt-in screen, where users choose which data other attendees can see. Tone: friendly, clear, and reassuring, not "salesy". Keep each under 30 words. Say exactly what information will be shared (Spotify username, top artists, and listening history for this specific artist) and what won't be shared (exact location and other playlists). Clarify that users can change this setting or turn it off at any time.

AI can write grammatically appealing sentences, but it can't empathize with your users' pain points or understand what worries them unless you share that information with it.

Watch out for

  • Uniformity: AI-generated layouts sometimes fall back on the most common and already-existing patterns. For a feature about meeting strangers, it's easy for AI to default to dating-app conventions like swipeable profile cards, which would inadvertently set the wrong expectations for a feature meant to help concert-goers find company, and it could make some users less comfortable opting in.
  • Accessibility errors: AI wireframes sometimes use low color contrast, might miss labels or use touch targets that are too small. These issues might likely be amplified in this design: Spotify uses a dark interface, where gray text on black often fails contrast requirements. Since users will likely use this feature on their phones in dark, crowded concert venues, it's strongly recommended to check every screen against WCAG before moving forward.
  • : Tempting as it is to always use AI in producing the first draft, please try sketching your own version of the opt-in screen before prompting, so you don't lose the ability to make your own sketches and designs from scratch. Afterwards, you can always use AI to expand on your designs instead of replacing them.

06 Deliver

Prototyping, Usability Testing & Handoff

This phase tests solutions with real users and hands off the final design to developers. At this stage, (and other AI tools) can take multi-step actions on their own as needed, and turn the design file into a working prototype or ready-to-ship code.

Who handles what during prototyping, testing, and handoff
AI can handleThe designer should own
Creating working prototypes from existing designs/conceptsDetermining what the prototype needs to test
Drafting usability study scripts and proposing tasksOverseeing sessions and taking note of what users do and say
Summarizing test findings and identifying issuesPrioritizing issues in terms of urgency: what "MUST" be fixed vs. what "would be nice" to fix (must-haves vs. nice-to-haves)
Generating handoff code and documentationTesting with assistive technology to ensure accessibility

Sample prompt

Here are notes from the 5 usability sessions we've conducted testing the Concert Connect Prototype. Participants were asked to find Concert Connect from a concert listing, opt in while choosing what to share, review a potential match, and send a message in the group chat. List every usability issue observed, how many participants experienced it, and its severity (critical, major, minor) based on whether it prevented task completion. Separate what participants did from what they said. List any issue related to privacy or safety separately, even if it seems minor.

Watch out for

  • Polished vs. validated: An AI-built prototype may appear finished if it features realistic profiles and concert listings. This may give stakeholders a false impression that the flow works before anyone even tests whether users understand what "Listening history for Lumen Bay" means or feel comfortable meeting a concert match.
  • What users say vs. what they do: It's crucial to remember that participants can, at times, say one thing but do another. A user might say they're comfortable sharing their top artists, then take 50 seconds deciding whether to turn the toggle on. AI overviews can sometimes report the user's verbal comment but overlook their contrasting behavior, which is why the prompt asks for these two to be examined separately.
  • AI-generated code: AI-generated code often fails accessibility checks. For the opt-in screen, a potential issue is toggles built from plain visual elements might prompt a screen reader to interpret it as a "button" instead of "First name, off." Developers and designers should review generated code and ensure that it's tested with a screen reader.
  • Agents acting without review: It's important to keep in mind that the AI is a 'junior partner' so while it can push copy changes, edit the design file, or share prototype links, there have to be clearly-established checkpoints where a person approves before anything goes live.

08

Conclusion

At the start of this project, I posed the question on whether UX Designers will still be relevant in two or three years, given how fast AI is advancing. Having worked through each stage of the UX Design process, my answer is a resounding "YES", but it's worth noting that the designer's place is rapidly shifting. AI can draft, summarize, wireframe, and even build, but what it can't do is care about the person on the other side of the screen, notice cues in their body language that might indicate something feels off, or take responsibility when a design simply falls short of users' needs and expectations.

This is the designer's job, and it's arguably the most important part of the whole process, because what good does a fancy design do if no one asked for it to begin with or has any idea how to navigate it? As AI helps out with the execution part, the value of the designer is heavily centered around judgment and asking the right questions to ensure that the right problem is being solved and that the user remains at the center.

Glossary

Affinity Diagramming
A method for organizing research observations into groups based on shared themes.
Agentic AI
AI systems that can plan and carry out multi-step tasks with some independence, such as navigating software or editing files.
AI Hallucination
When an AI produces false or invented information and presents it as fact.
AI Slop
Low-quality, generic AI-generated content produced in volume without meaningful human review.
Context Window
The amount of text an AI model can consider at one time; information beyond it may be lost or ignored.
Deskilling
The loss of skills that happens when people rely on automation for tasks they used to do themselves.
Double Diamond Framework
A design process model with four phases (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver), alternating between broad exploration and narrow focus.
Generative AI (GenAI)
AI that creates new content, such as text, images, or code, based on patterns in its training data.
HITL (Human-in-the-Loop)
An approach where people stay involved in reviewing and approving AI decisions.
Information Architecture (IA)
The structure and organization of content so users can find what they need.
Microcopy
Short interface text, such as button labels, error messages, and form hints.
PII (Personally Identifiable Information)
Any data that could identify a specific person, such as names, emails, or addresses.
Prompt Engineering
The practice of drafting and polishing instructions to get useful output from AI.
WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines)
International standards for making web content accessible to people with disabilities.
